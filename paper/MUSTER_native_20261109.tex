\begin{abstract}
An AI agent's memory stores differ in the structure they already record. Logs number their lines, example banks separate their records, and prose does neither. RAG keeps every word and can score well after heavy tuning to a dataset, but the tuning does not generalize. Model-built memory scales to long histories but is costly to build and can drop evidence. Agents need an autonomous strategy that is cheap to build and leaves little to tune. \sys{} detects each store's structure and builds memory from it. Two byte statistics, the shares of numbered lines and of text in records short enough to keep whole, set how a store is cut, grouped and ordered, with no model, labels, queries or candidate runs. \sys{} matches retrieval to that structure, which sets how dense, lexical and parent-graph channels split the slots. It assembles the context in stored order, keeping the newest version of each fact where an order is recorded. The rule for cutting and grouping, set on MemoryAgentBench, applies unchanged to LoCoMo and BEAM. On MemoryAgentBench the released implementation with the paper settings has the highest Overall among the retrieval and memory systems at the benchmark's reader ($60.5$ against $43.6$). Its per-store automatic configuration scores $58.5$. The original implementation is the strongest row on LoCoMo under both answer prompts ($86.04$ and $93.46$) with no generative call before the answer, where the strongest other system makes $9{,}398$. On LoCoMo-Refined it matches that system under the official evaluation ($77.57$ against $75.33$) and leads it by $8.5$ points with a reasoning reader. On BEAM, from 100K to 10M tokens, it matches the strongest system we compare at equal context and has the highest mean with an open-weight reader at 9.7K tokens. It builds a 10M-token memory in $77$ seconds, where model-built memories take hours. Read beside a model-built memory, it averages $73.1$ on BEAM at $19.5$K tokens, above either store alone and every system at that budget. \sys{} keeps each store as written and re-derives its configuration in seconds as the store grows.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}

An agent that answers from memory inherits a store it did not design. The store arrives as bytes (a
transcript, a list of assertions, a novel, a bank of labelled examples), and something must decide how to cut
it, how to group the pieces, and what to do when two pieces disagree. RAG keeps every word and can score well after heavy tuning to a dataset, but its recipe is hidden, and finding it takes labelled questions or candidate runs for every store. Model-built memory scales to long histories but pays before the first question and can drop evidence \cite{fidelity}. An agent that keeps logs, example banks and documents beside its conversations needs an autonomous strategy that is cheap to build, leaves little to tune and first asks whether a store already records the structure a reader needs.

In MemoryAgentBench \cite{mab}, fact-consolidation stores number their assertions,
in-context-learning stores separate their examples with blank lines, and narrative stores do neither. \sys{}
measures these properties (Section~\ref{sec:stats}, Figure~\ref{fig:rule}). \serial{} detects numbered records that
expose an update order, and \cov{} tells whether bounded records already make good retrieval units. The
two statistics see no question, gold answer or dataset name and call no model, and over fourteen stores they run
in three seconds and configure thirteen (LongMemEval keeps its sessions).

The memory systems we run are reported on conversation benchmarks alone, and their lead does not carry. The strongest other system we ran on LoCoMo scores $23.1$ on MemoryAgentBench, below BM25's $41.5$ (Table~\ref{tab:board}). The rule set on MemoryAgentBench derives the units and parents of LoCoMo, LoCoMo-Refined, BEAM and a model-built memory from their bytes, with no change to the rule. In the results of Section~\ref{sec:results}, \sys{} is the strongest memory system on each benchmark or matches it at equal context.

\paragraph{Detect before you construct.} Unlike True Memory \cite{truememory}, which reads every store with one fixed representation, and Adaptive Chunking \cite{adaptivechunk}, which picks a chunker by running every candidate, \sys{} derives a store's unit, parent, order and retrieval policy from two byte statistics, with no model and no candidate run. It \emph{detects} whether the store records the structure a reader needs, including an order, and sets the unit and hierarchy from it. It \emph{matches} a hybrid retrieval to that structure, giving the parent graph most slots on a numbered log and balancing it with dense and lexical search elsewhere. It \emph{assembles} the context in stored order, keeping the newest version of a numbered fact. An order the store does not record is worse than none. On fixed selections, shuffled serials score $59$ on one fact-consolidation cell, below $64$ with no order and $92$ with the recorded one (Appendix~\ref{app:slotsrc}). Deterministic conflict resolution \cite{freshness} assumes the serial from the task format and extracts facts with a model on every query, while \sys{} detects the serial and scores $93.0$ and $60.0$ on the fact-consolidation cells with the released implementation ($92.0$ and $37.0$ in the original), against the $82.0$ and $27.0$ it reports at the same reader.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=*,nosep]
\item \textbf{Detection before construction, for different kinds of agent memory.}
\sys{} configures a store's read path from two closed-form statistics of its bytes, with no model, labels, per-store queries or candidate runs. It re-derives that configuration in seconds as the store grows. The share of numbered lines tells whether the store records an order and only then turns on keep-newest resolution and parent-priority retrieval. The share of text in short records tells whether to use a finer cut at record boundaries. The detected structure then sets how hybrid retrieval splits the slots among its channels, and assembly keeps stored order.
\item \textbf{First place on MemoryAgentBench.}
The released implementation with the paper settings scores $60.5$ against $43.6$ at the benchmark's GPT-4o-mini reader and has the highest Overall among the retrieval and memory systems in Table~\ref{tab:board} at that reader. \section{Related work}

\paragraph{Structure built at write time.} RAPTOR \cite{raptor}, GraphRAG \cite{graphrag}, HippoRAG \cite{hipporag,hipporag2} and MemoRAG \cite{memorag} spend a model per document before any query arrives, paying up front to scale to long histories. On MemoryAgentBench the strongest of them, HippoRAG-v2, ties a plain sparse retriever overall ($41.6$ against $41.5$) and trails it by $8.7$ on test-time learning (Table~\ref{tab:board}). When the store already records structure, reading it is cheaper, and checking which case holds costs seconds.

\paragraph{Memory systems compared.} Mem0 \cite{mem0}, Honcho \cite{honcho}, Hindsight \cite{hindsight} and EverMemOS \cite{evermemos}, which we run, write memory with a language model as each session arrives, at $4.4$K to $26.1$K language-model calls for one 10M-token conversation (Table~\ref{tab:beamscale}), and \sys{} none. True Memory \cite{truememory}, which we also run, writes with no generative call and reads every store with one fixed representation. Mem0 2.1.0 also adds BM25 and entity scores to its dense ranking \cite{mem0oss}. Table~\ref{tab:board} further reports MemGPT \cite{memgpt}, Zep \cite{zep} and MIRIX \cite{mirix}, and Section~\ref{sec:mabres} compares Infini Memory \cite{infini} under its own scoring.

\paragraph{Memory without a generative write.} Fidelity Before Structure \cite{fidelity} finds that verbatim chunks beat extracted artifacts and pools both in one list, whereas \sys{} gives each store its own share of the budget (Table~\ref{tab:extend}). EdgeMem \cite{edgemem}, like True Memory, builds memory with no generative call through one pipeline for every store, and LazyMem \cite{lazymem} defers construction to query time with a trained 4B model. \sys{} also writes with no generative call, trains nothing and adds detection, reading the configuration from each store's bytes.

\paragraph{Choosing the cut, resolving conflicts, assembling.} Mix-of-Granularity \cite{mog}, AutoRAG \cite{autorag}, HiChunk \cite{hichunk}, FluxMem \cite{fluxmem} and Adaptive Chunking \cite{adaptivechunk} search for each store's RAG recipe by running every candidate, by labelled answers or by a trained model. OP-RAG \cite{oprag}, SeCom \cite{secom} and MacRAG \cite{macrag} assemble by order or by parent over preset or model-cut units, and other 2026 work resolves conflicts once they are known \cite{supersede,statefuse,tepa,lattice,toki,memlace,memtxn}. \sys{} instead measures two byte statistics before retrieval and sets the unit, the parent and the order from them, including whether an order exists at all, and resolves conflicts as a read-time view (Appendix~\ref{app:slotsrc}).

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering

\sys{}'s three parts form one flow (Figure~\ref{fig:read}, Algorithm~\ref{alg:muster} in Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}). \emph{Detection} sets unit, parent, order and channel floors from two byte statistics in seconds. \emph{Structure-matched hybrid retrieval} reserves each channel its floor, most slots for the parent graph on a numbered log. \emph{Context assembly} shows the selection in stored order, the newest version of each slot where an order is recorded. We call a retrieval policy an \emph{arm}. Detection sees no dataset name, question or gold answer, so the rule set on MemoryAgentBench runs unchanged on LoCoMo, BEAM and a model-built memory.

\subsection{Detection: byte statistics}\label{sec:stats}

A retriever needs a unit, a parent and an order, and before the first query the only store content it may consult is the bytes. Two byte properties decide these choices. Whether an order exists is decided by whether the store counts. A leading integer on a line is the simplest surface form a writing process appends, and the rule takes a larger one as a later write. Whether the store may be cut finer depends on how much of its text lies in records that fit the reference chunk. Neither statistic needs a query, a label or a model, and \cov{} is measured against the cut the benchmark would otherwise make, which lets one threshold license a finer cut on a record store and refuse it on a novel.
\begin{align}
\serial &= \frac{\#\{\ell \in L : \ell \text{ matches } \langle\textit{integer}\rangle\texttt{.}\ \langle\textit{body}\rangle\}}{|L|},
\qquad L = \text{non-empty lines}, \\
\cov &= \frac{\sum_{r \in R,\, |r| \le c} |r|}{\sum_{r \in R} |r|},
\qquad R = \text{blank-line records},\ |r| = \text{tokens in } r,
\end{align}
where $c$ is the reference scale, the chunk size the benchmark prescribes for the cell and the one operating
input the rule takes besides the bytes.
\serial{}, short for \emph{serial fraction}, asks whether the store counts. High means the store recorded its own order, and low means the rule claims no order to resolve by. Numbered lines do not push a conversation to the threshold. In the 65 BEAM conversations and the 500 LongMemEval-S histories \cite{longmemeval} they are $15\%$ to $35\%$ of the lines on average and at most $45\%$, and no store reaches $0.80$. \cov{}, short for \emph{record share}, asks whether the store is made of
records rather than merely containing some. With a high share, the cut follows record boundaries and slices records longer than the unit.
With a low share, the store is narrative and a finer cut would be arbitrary. \cov{} weights records by their tokens. Counted by records, InfBench-Sum would join the record stores, though two of its records hold $45.0\%$ of its tokens (Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}).


\subsection{Detection: derivation rule}\label{sec:rule}

\begin{quote}
If $\serial \ge 0.80$, the unit is the record with its serial stripped, the parent is the record's
normalised slot, and the order is the serial. Otherwise the unit is the chunk size the benchmark
prescribes. When $\cov \ge 0.90$, the rule refines the unit by one documented step ($4096\to512$)
and cuts at record boundaries, slicing only records longer than the resulting unit (the record-preserving cut).
In the non-serial case, the parent is eight times the unit, halved until at least ten parents remain, and
no order is claimed.
\end{quote}

On MemoryAgentBench, the original implementation applies this rule to the first up to three stores of each cell and uses the resulting representation throughout that cell. The released SDK's paper-settings configuration preserves these automatically derived cell representations, while Auto derives a plan separately for each store; Appendix~\ref{app:hyper} gives the channel and rendering choices.

We follow \textsc{derive} (Figure~\ref{fig:read}) end to end on two stores. FactConsolidation's store arrives as
lines of the form \texttt{1. Alice's employer is Zynga}, so \serial{} reads $0.9999$. The unit becomes one
record with the serial stripped, the parent a slot key naming the record's entity and attribute, read by a
regular expression (Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}), and the order the integer itself. Nothing is chunked and nothing is embedded to decide it. The parent map is a real
partition, so \emph{resolve} has something to resolve by. DetectiveQA's store is a novel, where \serial{}
is $0$ and \cov{} is $0$, so the unit is the benchmark's own $4096$-token chunk, the parent is eight of those
halved until at least ten remain, and no order is claimed, which switches supersession off rather than
letting keep-newest delete by an arbitrary key.

One cell is outside the rule. The benchmark's loader delivers LongMemEval as conversation rounds grouped
into sessions, and we keep that segmentation. The rounds are the units and the sessions the parents. Both
statistics are still computed for it, and neither fires, but the rule's unit and parent are not applied.
For the other thirteen cells, the rule determines the representation.

Besides the two thresholds, the rule's three constants come from the benchmark. They are the two chunk sizes it uses, $512$ and $4096$, their ratio of eight as the parent multiplier, and its retrieval budget of ten as the floor on parents (Table~\ref{tab:hyper}). Section~\ref{sec:results} therefore tests a fixed rule.

\subsection{Detection: policy gate}\label{sec:gate}

A graph-heavy arm wins where the parent map
carries meaning, and a dense-heavy one wins where it does not. The gate reads \serial{}, the statistic that
already chose the representation. A store where it fires takes the parent-priority arm, and every other store
the balanced one (Section~\ref{sec:read}). \serial{}'s two classes are $0.998$ apart, so any threshold in the gap gives the same partition. A model-based meaning test agrees with the gate on all fourteen stores (Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}).

\subsection{Structure-matched hybrid retrieval: reach and channel floors}\label{sec:read}

Retrieval is a hybrid of three channels over the derived units (a dense embedding index, BM25, and a parent-level graph joining
units that share an entity), and one cross-encoder scores whatever they
return. Ranked by one shared score, the pooled candidates
let strong dense recall take almost every slot, and lexical or structural evidence that would answer the
harder questions never reaches the reader. \emph{Reach} therefore gives each channel a \emph{reserved floor}, a minimum number of selected units drawn from its own ranking. The shared score fills the slots, and a channel left short trades the weakest selected unit outside it for its own next candidate.

At $U{=}10$ units the default arm, \balanced{}, sets the floors $(f_d,f_s,f_g)=(4,3,3)$ and, in the paper configuration for MemoryAgentBench's record cells and LongMemEval, admits a fourth channel at floor zero, the immediate neighbours of each seed inside its own parent, which enters only on its score. \parprio{} inverts the emphasis at $(2,0,8)$. Two dense anchors represent the query, and the parent graph spends the rest on breadth. Where the derived parent map has semantic meaning, that breadth is useful, and otherwise the balanced arm avoids paying for it (Table~\ref{tab:gate}).

\subsection{Context assembly: read-time supersession and containers}\label{sec:resolve}

Assembly shows the selection in stored order. Where an order was derived, \emph{resolve} replaces each selected unit by the newest unit of its slot in the store, keeps one unit per slot and refills freed slots from the reranked dense candidates in rank order, without re-checking floors (Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}), so the context shows distinct current evidence rather than repeated versions of one slot. This is the \emph{prune} view (Appendix~\ref{app:moved}). Without a derived order, resolution is off. A wrong order can cost only units that share a slot (Appendix~\ref{app:slotsrc}).

Resolution happens at read time and is discarded with the context, so the store is never rewritten and a
wrong call never deletes evidence from later queries. Write-time consolidation amortises its decision, and
its error, over every later query.

The compensated refinement branch spans the three parts. Detection takes it for a $4096$-token unit, retrieval searches at $1024$ tokens and selects four times as many units, and assembly shows them one container per parent in stored order. The nominal allowance is unchanged at $40{,}960$ tokens, so the branch raises retrieval resolution rather than buying more reader context (Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}).

\paragraph{The released implementation.} On dot-numbered stores, the SDK groups qualifying records by subject and relation, leaving values out of the slot key. A relation must occur for at least two subjects, with changing subjects averaging at most four records. Other records keep the original slot rule. The question seeds subjects whose current values name further subjects. The read path follows these links for at most four rounds, ranks each round and presents the selected chain in hop order. Across these rounds it reranks at most $400$ walk candidates, in addition to the dense and parent-graph candidates already scored. Other numbering formats keep the original read path. SDK (paper settings) and Auto use this same numbered-fact path. Their complete reader inputs on the $200$ FC questions are identical, so the two configurations reuse the same responses and have the same FC scores. Appendix~\ref{app:hyper} states the evaluation and development conditions.

\section{Setup}\label{sec:setup}

We evaluate all fourteen cells of MemoryAgentBench, an aggregate of separate public datasets whose stores span $25$ units for a detective novel to $18{,}333$ for an assertion log, a range of $733\times$ that one threshold pair has to cross without per-dataset tuning (Table~\ref{tab:stores}). We keep the benchmark's four competencies, accurate retrieval (AR), test-time learning (TTL), long-range understanding (LRU) and selective forgetting (SF), the last being the conflict-resolution family this paper turns on. Each cell keeps its own metric, substring match except GPT-4o judgment for LongMemEval and InfBench-Sum and recall@5 for Recsys. GPT-4o-mini \cite{gpt4omini}, GPT-4.1-mini \cite{gpt41} and GPT-5-mini \cite{gpt5} each answer $1{,}671$ questions (Table~\ref{tab:stores}), and Overall averages the four competency scores as \cite{mab} defines them. Mem0 2.1.0 \emph{full-ver.}\ reads its top $100$ extracted memories as the benchmark reads Mem0. Its raw-chunk row (the benchmark's chunks stored without extraction) and Honcho, Hindsight, EverMemOS and True Memory are cut at \sys{}'s per-question budget, never below one whole unit, and on FactConsolidation each reads its own uncut retrieval (Appendix~\ref{app:beamscale}).

Twelve cells are also rescored with Infini Memory's GPT-5 binary prompt \cite{infini}, keeping Recall@5 and the summarisation judge.
The two statistics and their thresholds were fixed before the rule's runs. The read-path settings (floor
triples, view, $K_1$, encoder input length) were set on MemoryAgentBench and frozen for LoCoMo and BEAM (Appendix~\ref{app:fixed}).

\section{Conclusion}

RAG keeps every word and can score well after heavy tuning to a dataset, but the tuning does not generalize. Model-built memory scales to long histories but pays up front and can drop evidence. \sys{} finds the recipe in each store's structure, with neither per-store tuning nor a language model at write time. It detects that structure from the bytes, including whether an order exists, matches retrieval to it and assembles the context in stored order. Set on MemoryAgentBench, \sys{} ranks first among the retrieval and memory systems at the benchmark's reader. With the same derivation rule, it is the strongest row on LoCoMo under both answer prompts and matches the strongest system on LoCoMo-Refined, with no generative call before the answer. On BEAM it matches the strongest system we compare at equal context and builds a 10M-token memory in $77$ seconds, where model-built memories take hours. By the same rule it also reads memories a language model has extracted, and on BEAM reading them beside the conversation gives a higher mean than either alone. Because \sys{} keeps each store as written and re-derives its configuration in seconds as the store grows, we expect it to serve the memory and long contexts of many AI agents.

\small
\caption{\sys{}. Part~I runs detection, including the policy gate (step~11), once per plan with no model. On MemoryAgentBench, the original implementation derives a representation for each cell from its first up to three stores. Part~II (retrieval and context assembly) uses one query embedding, an initial rerank and one reader call per query. The SDK's numbered-fact walk can add rerank rounds. $\tau_{\mathrm{num}}=0.80$ and $\tau_{\mathrm{rec}}=0.90$, and $P$ is the benchmark's chunk ($512$ or $4096$, Table~\ref{tab:hyper}). LongMemEval keeps its delivered rounds and sessions as units and parents (Section~\ref{sec:rule}).}
\label{alg:muster}
\footnotesize
\textbf{Input:} store bytes $S$ (never modified), query $q$. \textbf{Output:} the context.
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{\textsc{Part I, Derive}} \emph{(once per plan)}
\STATE $\serial \gets$ share of lines opening with a serial marker; $\cov \gets$ share of text inside bounded records
\IF{$\serial \ge \tau_{\mathrm{num}}$}
  \STATE unit $\gets$ record, parent $\gets$ slot, order $\gets$ serial
\ELSIF{$\cov \ge \tau_{\mathrm{rec}}$}
  \STATE unit $\gets$ record-preserving cut of $P/8$ tokens (at least $512$)
\ELSE
  \STATE unit $\gets$ sentence-bounded cut of $P$ tokens; if $P>512$, refine to $P/4$ and show $4\times$ the units
\ENDIF
\STATE \textbf{if} $\serial < \tau_{\mathrm{num}}$: parents $\gets$ $8\times$ the unit, halved until at least ten parents remain
\STATE channel floors $\gets$ parent-priority $(2,0,8)$ if $\serial \ge \tau_{\mathrm{num}}$, else balanced $(4,3,3)$
\STATE \textbf{\textsc{Part II, Read}} \emph{(once per query)}
\STATE candidates $\gets$ dense $\cup$ lexical $\cup$ parent walk, each channel with a floor (when enabled, also each seed's neighbours in its parent, floor $0$)
\STATE select the top units after the initial reranking pass; lift any channel below its floor\label{alg:lift}
\IF{order} \STATE replace each unit by the newest unit of its slot in the store, keep one per slot and refill freed slots in rank order (a view, not a write) \ENDIF
\STATE assemble in stored order, one container per parent when parent-grouped rendering is used; \textbf{return} the context
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Two properties of the reserved floors carry the design. A floor is filled from the channel's \emph{own}
ranking rather than credited to any candidate that also sits in that channel's top-$K$. Under the looser
reading a dense unit lying deep in BM25's ordering satisfies the sparse floor by overlap and the lexical
best never enters. Under \balanced{}, floors are enforced after the global selection, largest floor first. The evicted unit is the lowest-scoring selected unit outside the channel whose removal keeps every other channel at its count, with ties broken by unit identifier. Candidate selection deduplicates unit identifiers, and the graph uses parents, but the balanced floors do not guarantee distinct parents.

\paragraph{Finer search, assembled context.}
Small units can retrieve a precise fact while omitting context needed to interpret it. A non-serial store
that keeps a $4096$-token unit under the rule is searched at $1024$ tokens with $U=40$
selected units. Floors scale by $\alpha=\max(1,\lfloor U/10\rfloor)$ to $(16,12,12)$, and the parent
scale is recomputed by the same multiplier rule, giving $8192$ tokens on the evaluated stores.
This branch uses the three channels without the extra neighbour source and applies to EventQA,
InfBench-Sum and DetectiveQA. LoCoMo, LoCoMo-Refined and BEAM also use the three channels without it. The other branches keep their policies, including parent-priority
on serial stores. LongMemEval uses its native rounds and sessions rather than the token chunker (Section~\ref{sec:rule}).

On the refined branch the forty selected units are shown one container per parent ($8192$ tokens on the
evaluated stores), labelled \texttt{Memory $i$:}. A parent is never split across containers, and the containers and
the units inside each follow stored order. LoCoMo, LoCoMo-Refined and BEAM show their selected units the same way, at their own unit counts and parent sizes. On the other branches of MemoryAgentBench the ten selected units are shown as they are, in
stored order and without labels. The nominal content allowance remains $40\cdot1024=10\cdot4096=40{,}960$ tokens,
while actual prompt length and execution cost can differ. Ten is the benchmark's \texttt{retrieve\_num}, which its
own prompt assembly turns into ten labelled containers for the sparse, dense and graph baselines alike. We fetch
the same allowance but show as many containers as the selection has parents, the count Appendix~\ref{sec:items}
measures. The alternative merges the forty units into at most ten containers ordered by rank, and Appendix~\ref{app:more} compares the two on MemoryAgentBench.
The refined branch separates the resolution of retrieval from the context assembled for the reader.

A write-time extractor needs one generative call per unit, $6{,}151$ across the twelve chunked
stores alone, and the constructors in Table~\ref{tab:board} spend one per document.

The dense encoder is BAAI/bge-m3 \cite{bgem3} in fp32, the sparse channel is BM25 and
the cross-encoder is BAAI/bge-reranker-v2-m3 \cite{bgererank}. All runs set the first-stage parameter $K_1=200$, and the encoder and reranker
read at most $2048$ tokens of a \sys{} unit (shorter units are unaffected). The first row of Table~\ref{tab:gate} reads each $4{,}096$-token chunk whole, so its control sees every chunk in full. Reader identifiers are \texttt{gpt-4o-mini-2024-07-18},
\texttt{gpt-4.1-mini-2025-04-14} and \texttt{gpt-5-mini}. The first two answer at temperature $0.7$ with
seed $42$, the harness default. The third, \texttt{gpt-5-mini}, is a reasoning model that runs at its default sampling
with no output cap, and its answer is scored in full.
The Infini-style binary accuracy judge returned \texttt{gpt-5-2025-08-07}.
Reusing a judgment requires equality of the full question, reference, prediction and request settings.
Cell identity is read from each result file's recorded question type and parquet row, never from its
filename, and scoring runs through one entry point that owns the metric map, so a cell can neither be
scored by the wrong metric nor filled from a run it did not come from.

\paragraph{Rules of the original implementation.} The serial pattern is
\verb|^\s*(\d+)\.\s+(.+)$|. On a serial store the slot key is the record's first capitalised entity
span, lower-cased, joined to the record body in which every capitalised span is masked to \texttt{\_}.
Appendix~\ref{app:more} reports three coarser keys. Entities everywhere are capitalised spans found by a
regular expression, minus hubs, the entities that appear in more than $5\%$ of units. The graph channel
represents each parent by one unit, drops entities shared by more than $5\%$ of parents, seeds the parents that hold a query entity with that entity's IDF,
walks a bounded number of hops with weight decay $0.5$ per hop, orders the reached parents by hop and
then accumulated IDF with ties by stored order, offers the first $100$, and scores their representatives
with the shared cross-encoder. On a serial store the walk runs over slots. \emph{Resolve} replaces each selected unit by the largest-order unit of its parent in the store and keeps one unit per parent. It then refills from the reranked dense candidates in order, replacing each the same way and skipping parents already shown, without re-checking floors. On a serial store each kept unit is shown as \texttt{[serial] text} in serial order.

\paragraph{Why \cov{} weights records by their tokens.} Counted by records, InfBench-Sum, measured on its first three novels, would join the record stores on $4{,}539$ records, though two of them hold $45.0\%$ of its tokens. Weighted, the fourteen stores split eight at $0.9751$ or above and six at $0.5504$ or below. Computing both statistics and applying the rule to all fourteen stores takes $2.8$ seconds on a CPU after the stores are read (mean of three runs).

\paragraph{The gate's meaning test.} A meaning test, asking GPT-4o-mini whether the competing values of sampled slots are mutually exclusive, agrees with the form test of Section~\ref{sec:gate} on all fourteen stores, with its classes $0.147$ apart. The test measures InfBench-Sum on its first three novels and costs $\$0.12$ in all. The partition thus holds at two levels of description, and only the form test is free.

\paragraph{Floors in priority order.} Under \parprio{} the two dense anchors are filled first and the parent graph then spends the remaining budget on breadth. A neighbour under \balanced{} is never reserved a slot.

\paragraph{The released library's native path.} On dot-numbered stores, version 0.1.2 forms recognised subject and relation slots separately from their values. From question matches it follows value-to-subject links and orders the selected records by hop. For these FC rows it selects ten units. At most four walk rounds and $400$ walk candidates are a separate search budget in addition to the initial retrieval. Other numbering formats retain the original read path. Slots are matched by text pattern. These rules were developed using the $600$ FactConsolidation questions at the smaller scales and the reported $262$K stores.

\paragraph{Library evaluation conditions.} SDK (paper settings) in Tables~\ref{tab:board}, \ref{tab:expanded4o-mini}, \ref{tab:expanded4.1-mini}, \ref{tab:expanded5-mini} and~\ref{tab:expandedfrontier} is the released implementation in native mode with the paper's cell configuration held fixed. That configuration preserves the cell representations originally derived from the first up to three stores per cell. Auto uses per-store derivation in the same SDK at the cell's protocol chunk size $P$ and retains LongMemEval's rounds adapter. The original record branches use the balanced arm with neighbours and flat rendering on MemoryAgentBench, but the three channels without neighbours and parent-grouped rendering on LoCoMo and BEAM. On its record and prose branches, Auto uses neighbours and parent-grouped rendering. The two detection statistics therefore do not specify every channel and rendering choice across benchmarks. On MemoryAgentBench, relative to the paper configuration, Auto groups the retrieved units of SH-QA, MH-QA, the five in-context-learning cells and Recsys by parent, under one \texttt{Memory n:} header per parent, and enables the neighbour channel on EventQA, InfBench-Sum and DetectiveQA. Auto chooses InfBench-Sum's branch per novel, changing it on $74$ of the $100$ novels. Both paths use the original questions, reference answers, reader settings and MemoryAgentBench rubrics, with the encoder and reranker capped at $2048$ input tokens. We read anew when no archived answer matches the full messages and request settings. Judgments are reused only after matching the full judge payload. On FC, the two configurations have identical complete inputs and reuse the same updated-path responses. DetectiveQA retains three reads per question. The Infini-style columns keep the original implementation's values. The SDK (paper settings) columns of Table~\ref{tab:expandedfrontier} use this native path with each reader's original high-effort and empty-answer fallback contract. Ancillary per-cell CSVs accompany the source.

\section{BEAM across scales: instrument and prompt}\label{app:beamscale}

\textbf{Instrument.} GLM-5.3-Flash answers at maximum reasoning effort through one
pinned provider. The judge is BEAM's official code, unmodified, with GPT-4.1-mini, and the score is the benchmark's
ten-category macro average, aggregated question $\rightarrow$ conversation $\rightarrow$ category.
Intervals come from a paired bootstrap over whole conversations with $10{,}000$ draws. The questions are $400$ over $20$
conversations at $100$K, $700$ over $35$ at $1$M and $200$ over $10$ at $10$M. Each message is written as one blank-line record holding the fields BEAM gives it (its id, date, session and turn index, speaker and text), and nothing else is added. The rule is read once per scale (pooled \cov{} $0.986$ at 100K,
$0.993$ at 1M and 10M). At every scale an answer left empty was re-asked with an output allowance of $31{,}200$ tokens ($30{,}000$ for reasoning) instead of $9{,}200$. Over the runs reported here, $149$ of $23{,}200$ GLM-5.3-Flash answers were re-asked at 100K, $413$ of $32{,}200$ at 1M and $232$ of $9{,}200$ at 10M (at most $16$, $48$ and $29$ in one run), and $6$, $7$ and $10$ of them stayed empty because reasoning used the larger allowance too. Qwen3.5-9B, read without reasoning, left no answer empty. Means and $\Delta$ in Tables~\ref{tab:samebudget}, \ref{tab:beamscale} and~\ref{tab:extend} are of unrounded scores, and the reader context of Table~\ref{tab:samebudget}, like that of Table~\ref{tab:beamscale}, is measured at 1M. True Memory's ranked results are cut to \sys{}'s two contexts, and its rows and \sys{}'s in Table~\ref{tab:beamscale} average two reads.

\textbf{Competitors.} Mem0 is the open-source release 2.1.0 \cite{mem0oss}, run as a local server with the
benchmark runner of its authors \cite{mem0bench}, returning the top $200$ memories with their dates. Its ingestion and search
differ from the system described in \cite{mem0}, which the Mem0 row of Table~\ref{tab:board} reports as
MemoryAgentBench measured it. The BEAM rows and the four MemoryAgentBench rows of release 2.1.0 are results of that release under this configuration. On MemoryAgentBench each $4{,}096$-token chunk of the benchmark is one \texttt{add} call, wrapped in the benchmark's memorize template for extraction or stored bare with \texttt{infer=False} for the raw-chunk row, and the six chunks longer than the embedder's input are split in equal parts. Honcho receives the same chunks, each as one user message followed by a short acknowledgement, is read after its ingestion queue has drained, with its session summary requested, and runs with dreaming off. EverMemOS runs its released code at \texttt{fe436d3} on the same chunks and acknowledgements, one conversation per source text, with GPT-OSS-120B (reasoning effort low) answering every language-model call of construction and retrieval and Qwen3-Embedding-4B and Qwen3-Reranker-4B serving retrieval, all on vLLM on one RTX PRO 6000 (the three retrieval calls, capped at $500$, $150$ and $300$ tokens, get $1{,}024$ more for the reasoning). One GPT-OSS-120B answer needed a parse-only repair, which fixes only the format of the answer's JSON and changes no prompt, model or retry. EverMemOS's $8{,}192$-token segment limit closes almost every MemCell at a chunk boundary ($5{,}733$ MemCells for $5{,}956$ chunks), and the context its answer stage assembles is cut at \sys{}'s per-question budget. Hindsight 0.10.3 retains each chunk as one item, one memory bank per source text in the text's order, with GPT-OSS-120B (reasoning effort low) on vLLM on one RTX PRO 6000 ($227{,}840$ facts from the $5{,}956$ chunks), and its recall is cut at \sys{}'s per-question budget. Mem0 2.1.0 full-ver.\ is read as the benchmark reads Mem0, with its top $100$ memories and no token cut ($1.0$K to $4.9$K tokens per cell with GPT-4o-mini writing and $0.06$K to $15.5$K with GPT-OSS-120B). Filled in its rank order to \sys{}'s per-question budget from its top $1{,}000$ instead, its whole store on the three novel cells, it scores $26.4$. The other competitor contexts are cut at \sys{}'s per-question budget, on average $4.3$K tokens on SH-QA and MH-QA, $4.4$K on Recsys, $5.7$K on LongMemEval, $5.3$K to $5.5$K on the five in-context-learning cells, $40.3$K to $40.5$K on EventQA, InfBench-Sum and DetectiveQA, and never below one whole unit of the system. On the two FactConsolidation cells that budget ($0.15$K) is smaller than one raw chunk, so each system reads its own uncut retrieval, on average $42.4$K tokens for Mem0's top ten raw chunks, $32.3$K for Honcho's context with no token limit, $11.6$K for Hindsight's default recall and $1.7$K for EverMemOS's full context. Raw chunks are the benchmark's $4{,}096$-token units, so a budget of $4.3$K to $5.7$K tokens holds one chunk, shown whole where it exceeds the budget. They are ranked by Mem0's search, which adds BM25 to the dense similarity of its embedder (text-embedding-3-small) and calls no reranker, and read by \sys{}'s reader and prompt. With \texttt{infer=False} no entities are linked, so the search's entity-match score is inactive. Mem0's default similarity threshold leaves some contexts empty, and they are read as such. On TREC-coarse and TREC-fine $10$ and $14$ contexts are empty for raw chunks, $1$ and $1$ with GPT-4o-mini writing, and $49$ and $15$ (and $3$ on FC-SH) with GPT-OSS-120B writing. In the contexts returned for the $500$ in-context-learning questions, GPT-4o-mini keeps a label in $0$ of $36{,}449$ memory lines and GPT-OSS-120B in $945$ of $21{,}383$ ($4.4\%$), and more than $99\%$ of the lines of either writer open with ``User''. None of the $200$ FactConsolidation contexts of either writer keeps a serial.

\textbf{Our answer prompt (system message).} The user message is the retrieved context followed by \texttt{QUESTION:}, the question
and \texttt{RESPONSE:}.

\begin{quote}\footnotesize
You are the user's personal assistant. You have a memory: excerpts from your earlier conversations with this user, retrieved for the current question. The memory is a record of what was said; the current question decides what to do with it.

\textbf{Sources}\\
\quad--\ Everything you state about the user - their projects, decisions, plans, preferences, dates, numbers, and what they told you - must come from the memory. Do not invent, embellish, or infer details the memory does not support.\\
\quad--\ If the question is about the user's own history or work and the memory does not cover it, say so plainly and name what is missing, instead of guessing or answering with related material. Do not fill such a gap with general knowledge.\\
\quad--\ If the question is a general request - an explanation, a calculation, a recommendation, a how-to - you may use your general knowledge to carry it out, shaped by what the memory says about this user.

\textbf{Reading the record}\\
\quad--\ The memory has an order: messages carry serial numbers, and some carry a date and a session/turn index; larger numbers were said later. Use this order for anything about sequence, timing, or what is current.\\
\quad--\ When a later statement updates or replaces an earlier one, the latest is current; bring up the earlier one only when the question is about the history or the change.\\
\quad--\ When two statements cannot both be true and neither is framed as a change, do not silently pick one: say what each of them says and ask the user which is right.\\
\quad--\ Combine excerpts from different parts of the record when the question needs it. When the answer involves dates, durations, counts, or order, state the specific dates or items you used and how you got the result.

\textbf{Answering the user}\\
\quad--\ Before answering a request, check the memory for how this user has asked to be answered - format, level of detail, examples, things to include or leave out - and for preferences or constraints that bear on the request. Honor them even when the current question does not repeat them.\\
\quad--\ Lead with the answer, and match its length to the question: a short factual question gets a direct one- or two-sentence answer; a request for a summary, explanation, plan, or comparison gets a complete, organized answer that covers each point with the specific details (names, versions, numbers, dates) from the memory.\\
\quad--\ Speak to the user directly and naturally. Refer to the memory as your earlier conversations with them, not as excerpts or numbered items.
\end{quote}

\section{LoCoMo: protocol and further results}\label{app:locomo}

\paragraph{Data and store.} We use LoCoMo's \texttt{locomo10.json} and its four answerable categories, numbered as LoCoMo numbers them, with $282$ multi-hop, $321$ temporal, $96$ open-domain and $841$ single-hop questions. The fifth category has no gold answer. Each turn is written as one blank-line record holding its dialogue id, its session date, the speaker, the text and, where the turn shares an image, the image caption, and nothing else is added. As for BEAM, $c=4096$ because LoCoMo prescribes no chunk. Two other ways of writing the same conversations, LoCoMo's own (a date header per session, then \emph{X said}) and one speaker-prefixed line per turn, give the same branch with $1{,}024$-token parents.

\paragraph{Floors below ten units.} The balanced floors $(4,3,3)$ fill ten slots, so five units need smaller ones. Below ten units we keep the two dense anchors, give each other channel one slot, and share the rest in the ratio $4{:}3{:}3$ by largest remainder, ties going in the fill order (dense, graph, BM25). Five units get $(3,1,1)$. The same rule returns $(4,3,3)$ at ten units and $(4,3,3)\cdot U/10$ at every multiple of ten, and with it switched on all $1{,}540$ contexts at ten units are byte-identical to those without it.

\paragraph{Prompts and judges.} Mem0's answer prompt is the single-list prompt of Mem0's LoCoMo evaluation code (\texttt{ANSWER\_PROMPT\_ZEP}, commit \texttt{aae5989}), because a \sys{} block holds both speakers. The judge is \texttt{ACCURACY\_PROMPT} of the same code. EverMemOS's answer prompt, context template and judge come from its evaluation code at commit \texttt{fe436d3}, and its answer is the text after \texttt{FINAL ANSWER:}. Readers and the GPT-4o-mini and GPT-4.1-mini judges run at temperature $0$. Claude Haiku 5.5 and GPT-5-mini (minimal reasoning) accept no temperature and run at the API's defaults. We report the mean of three runs per judge, one for GPT-5-mini.

\section{LoCoMo-Refined: protocol and further results}\label{app:refined}

\paragraph{Data.} LoCoMo-Refined \cite{locomorefined} (commit \texttt{887091190789}, CC BY-NC 4.0) holds LoCoMo's ten conversations ($272$ sessions, $5{,}882$ turns) and $1{,}382$ questions in four categories ($213$ multi-hop, $299$ temporal, $68$ open-domain and $802$ single-hop), $521$ of them marked multimodal. Against LoCoMo's $1{,}540$, $1{,}278$ keep their question and answer, $73$ keep the question with a new gold answer, $31$ are changed or new, and $178$ are dropped. In all, $163$ questions list more than one acceptable answer.

\paragraph{Stores and systems.} Every store is rebuilt from the benchmark's own conversation file. \sys{} applies the rule of Section~\ref{sec:method} unchanged and reads ten units. Mem0 2.1.0 runs as a self-hosted server and extracts with GPT-OSS-120B (reasoning effort low, served by DeepInfra through OpenRouter), every extraction prompt carrying its session date ($5{,}882$ calls, $5{,}359$ memories), and its list is cut to $4{,}900$ tokens. Hindsight 0.10.3 retains with the same model and service ($543$ calls) and is read at its default recall. True Memory 0.7.6.2 runs its Pro configuration with its HyDE expansion generated by the same model ($1{,}382$ calls) and without it, and its $100$ results are cut, in rank order, to $4{,}900$ tokens. EverMemOS runs its released code at \texttt{fe436d3} with GPT-OSS-120B (reasoning effort low, served by vLLM on one RTX PRO 6000) for construction and agentic retrieval, beside Qwen3-Embedding-4B and Qwen3-Reranker-4B, and is read at its ten retrieved memory cells ($582$ cells, with $6{,}940$ construction and $2{,}094$ retrieval calls counted together as on LoCoMo). HippoRAG-v2 runs as on LoCoMo, its graph and per-question fact filter on GPT-OSS-120B ($1{,}926$ calls), and is read at its ranked sessions filled to $4{,}900$ tokens. No system ingests an image's search query or URL. The query holds the gold answer in $75$ questions. Captions are kept where a system's loader takes them. Honcho runs as its hosted service under the LoCoMo runner of Appendix~\ref{app:locomo}, with no image URL or search query in any message's metadata, in new workspaces built from the benchmark's file ($5{,}882$ messages, a shared image's caption appended to its message, and one dream per speaker once the queue has drained). Honcho's context API is given $4{,}900$ tokens and returns $5.2$K, and Honcho writes with the service's own models, which are not version-pinned. The whole conversation is each conversation's stored text, unretrieved ($31.1$K tokens per question). One write model for every memory system but Honcho (Table~\ref{tab:writers}) follows the check on BEAM-100K (Appendix~\ref{app:beamscale}). There, writing with GPT-OSS-120B in place of the model of each system's paper moves Mem0 by $-0.2$ and EverMemOS by $0.0$ with GLM-5.3-Flash, and by $+1.0$ and $-1.3$ with Qwen3.5-9B under our answer prompt.

\paragraph{Answering and judging.} Every context fills the first memory slot of the answer template of the leaderboard's public LoCoMo-Refined pipeline \cite{aml}. Readers answer at temperature $0$. GPT-4o-mini (\texttt{gpt-4o-mini-2024-07-18}), the base model of every academic result on the leaderboard, answers through OpenAI's API, and the two readers of our BEAM runs (Section~\ref{sec:beamscale}), GLM-5.3-Flash at maximum reasoning effort and Qwen3.5-9B with reasoning off ($0$ reasoning tokens in every answer), answer through OpenRouter with one pinned provider each. Claude Sonnet 5.5 (\texttt{claude-sonnet-5-5}) answers through Anthropic's API at the API's defaults, as the model accepts no temperature, with adaptive thinking and at most $8{,}192$ output tokens. The official judge is Qwen3-14B (bf16, revision \texttt{40c06982}, temperature $0$, thinking off) served with vLLM 0.30.0. Qwen3-14B judges each acceptable answer in its own call and counts an exact match as correct without one. The AML judge, the official prompt with one added line and all acceptable answers in one call, is reported beside it. Paired comparisons use the exact two-sided McNemar test.

\section{Where the slots come from}\label{app:slotsrc}

On the two FactConsolidation cells, each question's selection (about ten records, each with its recorded
serial) is fixed, and only the source of the supersession slots changes. Unlike \sys{}'s prune view, which substitutes from the whole store (Section~\ref{sec:resolve}), the test resolves within the fixed selection. A record is dropped when one of
its facts has a newer value for the same slot in another record. The kept records are shown in ascending
serial with no refill, and GPT-4o-mini reads them at temperature $0$. The byte-read slot is the harness key
of Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}. The extractors see the same records, each cut to $600$ characters. One is an attribute-keyed prompt, ``From the numbered memory passages below, extract
facts ABOUT THE USER relevant to this question: $\langle q\rangle$. Output one per line exactly as:
attribute :: value :: passage\_number. Include superseded/older values too. Max 25 lines.'' with the lower-cased attribute as the slot, and the other an entity-aware prompt keyed by entity and attribute,
``From the numbered passages below, extract every fact relevant to this question: $\langle q\rangle$.
Output one per line exactly as: entity :: attribute :: value :: passage\_number. The entity is the
subject the fact is about. Include superseded/older values too. Max 25 lines.'' Without the refill of
\sys{}'s read path and at temperature $0$, the byte-read row scores $41$ on FC-MH where Table~\ref{tab:board}'s
refilled, temperature-$0.7$ run scores $37.0$.

\paragraph{The three views.}

The three views are produced in one run. \emph{Prune} keeps the newest unit per slot, refills, and
renders in ascending serial. \emph{Annotate} keeps every unit, in descending serial, and prefixes the
newest per slot with a current-value tag. \emph{Label} is annotate with the superseded units given a
superseded tag, so annotate and label share unit set, order and budget and both keep the history
that removal discards. Each unit carries its serial. \sys{} uses prune and shows each record as
\texttt{[serial] text}.

\subsection{One statistic, opposite choices}


A statistic that always picks the same unit cannot be checked, so Table~\ref{tab:refine} in
Appendix~\ref{app:moved} reports both choices. The table fixes $U=10$ units, before compensated refinement, and DetectiveQA is the mean of three reads on both sides. \cov{} licenses a finer cut on six of the eight cells and
refuses it on two. Of the six it wins three, is within $0.010$ on two and loses one, Recsys, most likely
because dialogue turns are short enough to fire the statistic while the preference a question needs
spans several turns. Paired over questions it separates on TREC-coarse, $+0.110$ on $17$
flips against $6$ ($p = 0.035$, exact two-sided sign test).

On the two cells where it refuses, keeping the protocol chunk is right by a wide margin at a fixed count. At $U=10$ finer units cost $0.040$ on EventQA and $0.089$ on DetectiveQA, with an eightfold
smaller allowance. The fixed-allowance test is the compensated branch, where EventQA gains $7.0$, $8.0$ and $12.0$ over ten $4096$-token units and reaches $82.0$ at all three readers. One statistic therefore produces opposite decisions and is correct in both directions at fixed
count.

\subsection{What the read path is worth, and where}

We test the read path apart from the statistics that configure it.
Table~\ref{tab:reach} in Appendix~\ref{app:moved} ablates it against the same candidates ranked by the
shared cross-encoder alone, with no reserved floors, no parent and no order, on the thirteen cells
where that instrument has a scored run, all but InfBench-Sum, with DetectiveQA the mean of three reads on both sides. The mean is $+0.044$, and two cells hold most of it. FC-MH's
$+0.250$ comes from the serial branch, where the parent map is a real partition and the order is
read. EventQA's $+0.160$ is what floors and the parent channels add together, since that
cell has no serial. Floors pay where the parent map provides useful
candidates, order pays only where a serial is available, and
where the map is degenerate a slot reserved for a channel with nothing to contribute displaces a
candidate the ranker preferred. A fixed policy pays this cost on every cell. \sys{} applies the parent-priority floors only where the bytes show a usable parent map.

Table~\ref{tab:gate} (Section~\ref{sec:ablations}) measures the same distinction from the policy side.
A fixed policy must choose which population to serve, which is why the two constants land at $56.0$ and $55.5$.
Parent-priority everywhere gains $6.0$ SF points and gives up AR ($70.3$ against $73.8$) and LRU ($39.5$ against $44.2$). Neither policy dominates, and the gate removes the need to choose one globally.

\subsection{How many items the reader should see}\label{sec:items}

The rank-ordered grouping stopped at ten containers because \texttt{retrieve\_num} is
ten, a constant that sets how many units to \emph{fetch}, not how many containers a
reader should be \emph{shown}. The two differ here. On InfBench-Sum the forty selected units touch $16.5$
parents on average ($8$ to $32$), so ten containers must merge parents, while one container per parent
keeps every container parent-pure.

At the same allowance, one container per parent instead of ten rank-ordered ones raises judged summarisation on all
three readers, by $+0.044$ ($61$--$35$, $p=0.010$, exact two-sided sign test), $+0.029$ ($52$--$48$, $p=0.76$) and $+0.037$ ($56$--$44$,
$p=0.27$) for GPT-4o-mini, GPT-4.1-mini and GPT-5-mini, and by $+0.036$ pooled ($95\%$ CI $[+0.006, +0.067]$,
$169$--$127$, $p=0.017$). A second read of the same contexts moves a reader's score by $0.003$ to $0.039$
(Appendix~\ref{app:items}), and all three readers gain. \sys{} therefore
shows one container per parent on the refined branch (Section~\ref{sec:resolve}).

\section{Re-read resolution}\label{app:items}

\paragraph{Resolution.} The readers sample (temperature $0.7$ for GPT-4o-mini and GPT-4.1-mini, $1.0$ for
GPT-5-mini), so a second read of byte-identical contexts is a second measurement. On InfBench-Sum two reads
of \sys{}'s contexts give no identical summary and judged F1 of $0.222$ against $0.219$ (GPT-4o-mini) and
$0.445$ against $0.417$ (GPT-5-mini). With ten containers they give $0.179$ against $0.198$ and $0.408$ against $0.369$.
DetectiveQA, read three times per reader on \sys{}'s contexts, moves by $1.4$ to $7.0$ points across reads
(Appendix~\ref{app:expanded}). Every DetectiveQA cell in this paper is therefore the mean of three reads. On
BEAM-100K two reads of \sys{}'s contexts score $75.3$ and $74.6$ at $U{=}20$ and $76.5$ and $75.8$ at
$U{=}40$, paired differences of $+0.7$ and $+0.6$, with $279$
and $284$ of $400$ questions scored identically. Table~\ref{tab:beamscale} reports the mean of the two.

\section{Where the settings come from}\label{app:fixed}

The two statistics (Equations~1 and~2), the thresholds $\tau_{\mathrm{num}}=0.80$ and $\tau_{\mathrm{rec}}=0.90$ and the factor of four of the compensated refinement branch (Section~\ref{sec:resolve}) were written down before the rule's runs. That branch reads four times as many quarter-size units, so the reader's allowance does not change. The read-path settings (the two floor triples, the prune view, $K_1=200$ and the $2048$-token encoder input) were set on MemoryAgentBench, whose questions are also the reported ones. LoCoMo and BEAM reuse them. The container rule of Section~\ref{sec:resolve} was compared on MemoryAgentBench (Appendix~\ref{sec:items}) and BEAM before it was fixed, and the two-store assembly of Table~\ref{tab:extend} was chosen among the alternatives of Appendix~\ref{app:beamscale}. Neither LoCoMo nor BEAM prescribes a chunk, so both take MemoryAgentBench's
$4{,}096$ tokens as $c$. LoCoMo reads ten units, the count MemoryAgentBench prescribes, and BEAM is reported at $20$, $30$ and
$40$ units with floors scaled to $U$. Our answer prompt (Appendix~\ref{app:beamscale}) was written on BEAM's $500$K
conversations, a scale we do not report.
